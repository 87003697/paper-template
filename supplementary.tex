\section{Analysis of Negative Source Guidance}
\label{sec:negative_source_guidance}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures_rebuttal/comparison_grid.png}
  \caption{\textbf{Editing trajectories with and without negative source guidance.}
  Starting from the same rendered source view $x^{\texttt{src}}$ and reference image $x^{\texttt{ref}}$, we visualize intermediate editing states and the final output.
  Without negative source guidance, the trajectory exhibits unstable global color changes and weaker local structures.
  With negative source guidance, Reinforced Editing stabilizes the reference-guided trajectory while preserving local details such as the helmet mark.}
  \label{fig:negative_source_guidance}
\end{figure}

Fig.~\ref{fig:negative_source_guidance} illustrates the role of the negative source branch in the Reinforced Editing differential velocity defined in the main paper.
When the branch is removed, the editing trajectory becomes less stable: colors are rapidly over-saturated, and local structures become weaker, as shown by the missing ``A'' on the helmet.
With the negative source branch, the reference-guided enhancement proceeds more smoothly and maintains more stable local markings throughout the trajectory.
This supports our use of the branch as an auxiliary regularizer for producing reliable pseudo-GTs during closed-loop 3D optimization.

\section{Human Preference Study Protocols}
\label{sec:human_study_details}
We conduct a human preference study to complement the automatic metrics in the main paper.
The study focuses on whether the generated 3D assets preserve the identity of the input image while improving visual fidelity across multiple rendered views.

\textbf{Study setup.}
We use the same 100 held-out examples from the Conceptual Design Dataset as in the main evaluation.
For each example, participants are shown the input reference image and multi-view renderings generated by three methods: Pretrained (Trellis), Photo3D, and OREO.
The method names are hidden during evaluation, and participants are asked to choose the result with the best overall quality.

\textbf{Evaluation criteria.}
Participants are instructed to consider three aspects jointly.
First, \textit{Fidelity} measures whether the generated asset contains realistic materials, sharp boundaries, and fine-grained texture details.
Second, \textit{Consistency} measures whether the rendered views remain coherent as a 3D object rather than showing view-dependent artifacts.
Third, \textit{Identity} measures whether the generated asset preserves the semantic identity and distinctive details of the input reference image.

\textbf{Participants.}
The study includes 20 participants, including participants familiar with 3D reconstruction and computer graphics.
All participants evaluate the same 100 examples, and we aggregate their preferences over all responses.

  \centering
  \caption{Human preference study on the held-out 100-example Conceptual Design Dataset. Participants compare multi-view renderings with respect to reference fidelity, 3D consistency, and identity preservation.}

    \toprule

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:human_preference}, OREO obtains the highest preference ratio.
This indicates that human evaluators more frequently prefer OREO's balance between reference fidelity, fine-grained visual details, and multi-view 3D consistency.
Since only aggregate preference ratios are recorded, we report the descriptive preference results and do not include unsupported significance tests.

\section{Implementation and Dataset Details}
\label{sec:implementation_details}
This section provides the implementation details used in our main experiments, including dataset construction, editing prompts, computational profiling, and adversarial training settings.

\subsection{Conceptual Design Dataset Construction}
\label{sec:dataset_construction}
We construct an image-only collection to support generator post-training without paired 3D supervision.
The dataset comprises around two thousand reference images collected from public online sources, with a focus on concept-design and stylized assets that exhibit rich appearance details and align with realistic user scenarios.
We manually filter the collection to remove duplicates, low-resolution thumbnails, watermarked images, and content with unclear foregrounds, prioritizing reference images with clean foregrounds, well-defined silhouettes, and informative texture or material cues.
For evaluation, we hold out a disjoint split of 100 reference images, each rendered into 5 query views by the pretrained Trellis backbone, yielding 500 evaluation pairs.
This held-out split is used as the Conceptual Design Dataset in the main paper and is never seen during training of either OREO or the Photo3D baseline.
We will release the curated reference images, the held-out evaluation split, and the editing prompts together with the source code.

\subsection{Editing Prompts}
\label{sec:editing_prompts}
We use a single fixed editing instruction for all 2D feedback sources to avoid prompt-specific tuning per example.
For Reinforced Editing as well as the off-the-shelf Qwen-Image-Edit and NanoBanana editors, we provide the rendered query view together with the conditioning reference image and the following instruction:
\begin{quote}
\itshape
``The first image is a 3D rendered view. The second image is the reference. Edit the first image to match the reference object's appearance. White background.''
\end{quote}
This prompt is used identically during training (for OREO post-training) and during evaluation (for the editing-feedback comparison in Sec.~4.1 of the main paper); only the underlying editor backbone is swapped.
API-specific parameters such as sampler, classifier-free guidance scale, and number of denoising steps are listed in the released source code.

\subsection{Computational Overhead}
\label{sec:computational_overhead}
OREO post-training augments standard generator updates with online 2D editing calls, which are the main source of additional compute.
We profile the system on a single node with 8$\times$H200 GPUs.
Without the editing component, one full training step takes approximately 22~s.
When the 9-step image editing process is executed sequentially on the same machine, it dominates the iteration cost and accounts for roughly 81\% of the per-step wall-clock time.
In practice, we wrap the editor as an asynchronous service and overlap each editing call with the rollout of the next training mini-batch, so that editing latency is hidden behind generator forward and backward passes.
With this overlap, the effective waiting time for editing drops to about 45\% of a training iteration on the same hardware, with no observed change in optimization dynamics or final quality.
The adversarial branch (Sec.~\ref{sec:adversarial_details}) adds only marginal extra cost (about 0.1~s per step), since the discriminator reuses a frozen DINOv3-S backbone.
Memory usage is dominated by the generator: a single H200 (140~GB) holds the model and activations within the available memory; activation checkpointing on the 3D generator further reduces the peak working set when needed for development on smaller cards.

\subsection{Benchmark Evaluation on GSO}
\label{sec:gso_details}
We additionally evaluate OREO on Google Scanned Objects (GSO), a widely used image-to-3D benchmark of real scanned everyday objects.
Whereas our Conceptual Design Dataset targets challenging in-the-wild user inputs, GSO provides a complementary distribution of common, in-domain assets that lie close to the pretrained backbone's training distribution.
We use 77 GSO objects and render each generated mesh from 16 viewpoints uniformly sampled along a full 360$^\circ$ azimuth orbit, conditioned on a single front-view RGB image.
We compare against the pretrained Trellis backbone and the Photo3D baseline using the same per-object conditioning images and the same rendering protocol.
Generation quality is reported with distribution-level metrics, FID and KID, computed against ground-truth GSO renderings (78 objects, 84 views per object) using the InceptionV3 feature extractor following the standard \texttt{clean-fid} implementation.

  \centering

  \label{tab:gso}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lcc@{}}
    \toprule
    Method & FID $\downarrow$ & KID $\downarrow$ \\
    \midrule
    Pretrained (Trellis) & 58.71 & 0.0081 \\
    Photo3D & 72.94 & 0.0137 \\
    OREO (Ours) & \textbf{57.32} & \textbf{0.0072} \\
    \bottomrule

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:gso}, OREO is the only configuration that improves over the pretrained Trellis backbone on both FID and KID, indicating that the fidelity alignment learned from out-of-distribution concept-design inputs also transfers to standard in-domain assets.
Photo3D, in contrast, shifts the appearance distribution further away from the GSO ground truth (FID $72.94$ vs.\ $58.71$), consistent with the qualitative observation in the main paper that its score-distillation supervision over-smooths textures.
These results complement the Conceptual Design Dataset evaluation in the main paper: OREO not only narrows the larger fidelity gap on out-of-domain inputs, but also yields measurable improvements on a conventional in-domain benchmark.

\subsection{Adversarial Training Details}
\label{sec:adversarial_details}
We implement the discriminator $D_\psi$ as a projected discriminator built on a frozen DINOv3-S image encoder (ViT-Small, 384-dim, 12 transformer blocks with register tokens).
The encoder parameters are kept frozen throughout post-training; lightweight trainable heads are attached to the intermediate features at hook depths $\{2,5,8,11\}$, skipping the CLS and register tokens, for multi-scale real/fake classification.
Each head is a small convolutional stack composed of a spectrally normalized $3{\times}3$ convolution, GroupNorm with 32 groups, LeakyReLU, and a residual block scaled by $1/\sqrt{2}$.
Inputs are bilinearly resized to $518{\times}518$ before entering the encoder.
During discriminator training, edited pseudo-GT views $x^{\texttt{tgt}}$ are treated as real samples, while rendered predictions $x^{\texttt{src}}$ are treated as fake samples.
The discriminator is updated once per generator step (1{:}1 alternation) using a binary cross-entropy objective with logits, and discriminator gradients are clipped at norm $1.0$ for stability.
For the generator update, the adversarial term encourages $x^{\texttt{src}}$ to be classified as real, complementing the pixel-level pseudo-GT loss with high-frequency appearance supervision.
We use generator learning rate $1{\times}10^{-4}$ and discriminator learning rate $2{\times}10^{-5}$ (five times slower than the generator), which we found necessary for stable adversarial training given the small number of trainable parameters in the projection heads.
The adversarial weight is $\gamma_{\text{adv}}=10^{-3}$; larger values caused the optimization to collapse, consistent with the conservative ablation discussion in the main paper.
We also experimented with R1 gradient penalty at $\gamma_{R1}\in\{0,10^{-4},10^{-3},10^{-2}\}$ and observed no measurable effect on either training stability or final metrics: the LayerNorm operations inside the DINOv3-S blocks attenuate the input-space gradient norm to roughly $10^{-10}$, rendering the R1 term inactive in practice, and we therefore disable it by default.
The frozen encoder is shared between forward and backward passes, so that only the multi-scale heads carry trainable parameters; this keeps the additional memory footprint and the per-step adversarial overhead small (Sec.~\ref{sec:computational_overhead}).
All real/fake pairs are formed online from the current closed-loop optimization step, so the adversarial branch does not require any additional real 3D supervision.

\section{Limitations and Failure Cases}
\label{sec:limitations_failure}
While OREO improves visual fidelity in most cases, its feedback signal is still derived from 2D editing priors rather than explicit 3D supervision.
This section discusses representative limitations observed during post-training.

\textbf{Viewpoint drift for face-like objects.}
When the rendered object contains a face or character-like front side, the 2D editor may prefer a canonical portrait composition.
As a result, the edited pseudo-GT can rotate or reorient the face toward the camera instead of preserving the intended forward-facing direction of the 3D object.
If such pseudo-GTs are repeatedly used during post-training, OREO may improve local facial details while introducing inconsistent face orientation across views.
This failure is caused by a mismatch between 2D portrait priors and the 3D view-consistency requirement.

\textbf{Inherited color and style bias.}
The dynamic noise update encourages the editing trajectory to follow the 2D editor's appearance prior more closely.
This generally improves texture richness and perceptual quality, but it can also inherit editor-specific color or style bias.
For objects with unusual materials, such as stone-like creatures, metallic surfaces, or highly stylized designs, the edited pseudo-GT may shift the intended hue or over-emphasize a dominant color tone.
When such biased pseudo-GTs are repeatedly used for generator optimization, the final 3D output may preserve the geometry but deviate in color distribution.

\textbf{Weakly constrained views and fine structures.}
OREO optimizes rendered views with online pseudo-GTs, but each editing query still observes a limited camera view.
Regions that are rarely visible or only weakly constrained, such as back views, occluded surfaces, thin structures, and sharp material boundaries, can show slightly mismatched texture density or local decorative details.
In such cases, OREO can improve the overall appearance while still leaving small artifacts around boundaries, appendages, or high-frequency ornaments.

These failure cases suggest that future work could incorporate stronger multi-view consistency constraints, 3D-aware editing models, or uncertainty-aware filtering of pseudo-GTs before they are used for generator updates.
